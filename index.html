In brief

Key facts

  • The F-1 is the most powerful single-chamber liquid-fuelled rocket engine ever flown: about 1.52 million lbf (6.77 MN) of thrust at sea level.
  • Five F-1s powered the Saturn V's first stage (S-IC), together producing roughly 7.6 million lbf at liftoff.
  • It burned RP-1 (a refined kerosene) with liquid oxygen at a mass ratio of 2.27 : 1 — about 69% of what it consumed was oxygen.
  • Each engine swallowed about 2.6 tonnes of propellant every second, fed by a single 55,000-horsepower turbopump.
  • The fuel cooled the chamber walls before it was burned, and the turbine's own exhaust shielded the nozzle extension.
  • 65 F-1 engines flew on 13 Saturn V launches between November 1967 and May 1973.
History

Apollo context

Rocketdyne began the F-1 in the late 1950s, initially for the US Air Force, before NASA took it on as the cornerstone of its heavy-lift plans. Its single huge chamber was a deliberate bet: fewer, bigger engines meant fewer parts to fail — if the engine could be tamed.

Taming it was the hard part. Early test firings suffered violent combustion instability, pressure oscillations that could destroy an engine in a fraction of a second. Engineers redesigned the injector with baffles and proved it by detonating small bombs inside running engines to check that disturbances died away.

On the Saturn V, the five F-1s burned for about two and a half minutes, lifting a vehicle of nearly 2,900 tonnes off the pad and through the thickest part of the atmosphere before the stage separated and fell into the Atlantic. In 2013 an expedition recovered F-1 components from the seabed.

  • Late 1950sRocketdyne starts the F-1; NASA soon adopts it for its large launch vehicles.
  • Early 1960sCombustion instability crisis; the baffled injector and bomb testing solve it.
  • 9 Nov 1967First flight on Apollo 4, the first Saturn V launch (uncrewed).
  • 16 Jul 1969Apollo 11 lifts off on five F-1s on its way to the first Moon landing.
  • 14 May 1973Final flight: Skylab 1, the last Saturn V.
